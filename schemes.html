Schemes of Arrangement 101

The legal machine behind Motherson's deal, explained on its own. What a scheme is, when bankers use one, how it moves through SEBI and the NCLT, the vote maths, tax and accounting, and four real Indian deals to compare.

01

What a scheme of arrangement is

A scheme of arrangement is a plan to reorganise one or more companies that the company proposes, its shareholders and creditors vote on, and a tribunal (the NCLT) then sanctions. Once sanctioned, it binds everyone in the classes that voted, including people who voted against or didn't vote.

Plain-English analogy

Think of a housing society that wants to merge with the society next door. You can't get 500 people to each sign a separate contract. So the committee drafts one plan, every member votes, a court checks it is fair, and then the plan applies to all 500 people at once. That is a scheme.

Section (Companies Act, 2013)What it covers
s.230Compromise or arrangement with members or creditors. The umbrella power, covering meetings, notices to regulators, and who may object.
s.231The NCLT's power to enforce and supervise the scheme after sanction.
s.232Mergers, amalgamations and demergers specifically: what the scheme must contain and the sanction order.
s.233Fast-track merger for small companies, a holding company with its wholly owned subsidiary, and startups. Approved by the Regional Director, with no NCLT hearing.
s.234Cross-border mergers (with RBI rules).
s.66Reduction of share capital, often bundled into schemes.

Why bankers love schemes

Consideration can be paid in shares, so no cash is needed. It is tax-neutral if the conditions are met. It binds every holder, so there are no hold-outs. It is generally exempt from the takeover open offer. And several steps can go into one composite scheme.

Why they are hard

They are slow (9 to 18 months), go through many regulators, and every step is public. Valuation is open to challenge, and in promoter-linked deals the public's vote can block them.

02

The main types, animated

Merger / amalgamation

Two companies become one. Shareholders of the company that disappears get shares of the survivor at a swap ratio. Example: HDFC Ltd into HDFC Bank.

Demerger

A business undertaking moves into a new or existing company, and the parent's shareholders get its shares. Usually mirror ownership. Example: Jio Financial, ITC Hotels, MSWIL.

Promoter holdco merger

The promoter's holding company merges into the listed company it owns. Its stake is cancelled and new shares go to the promoters directly. Example: SAMIL into MSSL.

Reverse merger

A larger (often unlisted) operating company merges into a smaller listed one to get a listing. SEBI scrutinises these closely.

Capital reduction (s.66)

Cancel or return share capital, for example to write off accumulated losses or to return surplus cash.

Creditor compromise

Lenders agree to take less, or to convert debt into equity, under a court-approved plan (outside the IBC).

Two-motion structure in one line

First motion: "Tribunal, please let us call meetings." Second motion: "Tribunal, the members voted yes. Please sanction the scheme." Between the two come the notices to regulators (30 days to respond) and the meetings.

05

The vote tests, with a calculator

Companies Act test (s.230(6))

Each class (shareholders, secured creditors, unsecured creditors) must approve by a majority in number of those voting, who also hold three-fourths in value of the votes cast.

SEBI majority-of-minority

For listed companies, where promoters or related parties benefit (for example a promoter entity merging in, or shares going to promoters), the public shareholders' votes for must exceed their votes against. Promoter votes don't count for this test.

Who can object

Only holders of at least 10% of shares, or creditors owed at least 5% of total debt, can object at the NCLT (s.230(4)). This stops tiny holders from blocking deals.

Skipping meetings

The NCLT can dispense with a creditors' meeting if creditors holding 90% in value consent by affidavit (s.230(9)). It dispensed with creditor meetings in Motherson's case.

Vote calculator (illustrative numbers)

Try making public AGAINST larger than public FOR. The scheme fails even though promoters vote 100% for it. That is the protection Motherson's minorities had (they actually voted 99.44% for).

06

The SEBI layer for listed companies

A listed company can't go straight to the NCLT. It first files the draft scheme with the stock exchanges, which consult SEBI and issue an observation letter (no-objection). The rules are in SEBI's master circular on schemes of arrangement by listed entities (June 2023), which consolidated the earlier 2017 circular Motherson followed.

Documents filed

Draft scheme; valuation report from a registered valuer; fairness opinion from a SEBI-registered merchant banker; audit committee report; report of the committee of independent directors (required since 2021); auditor's certificate on accounting; pre- and post-scheme shareholding; complaints report.

Listing a demerged company

The new company lists without an IPO, using a relaxation under Rule 19(2)(b) of the SCRR. It must publish an information document, not a DRHP. Ownership must largely mirror the parent, public holding must be at least 25%, and promoters' shares are locked in.

Investor protection

Majority-of-minority vote where promoters benefit; disclosure of valuation methods; a complaints window after filing; and exchange and SEBI comments that must be addressed before the NCLT.

Price discovery

On the ex-date of large demergers, exchanges can run a special pre-open session so the parent's price adjusts for the business that left. Jio Financial's 2023 demerger used one.

Common pitfalls
  • Valuation date at an extreme (a peak or a trough) invites challenge. Consider a true-up or explain the projections clearly.
  • Forgetting the order of steps in a composite scheme. A demerger mirrors ownership at that moment.
  • Under-communicating: publish accretion and look-through analysis early, before the stock reacts badly.
  • Ignoring post-deal related-party flows between the newly separated companies.
  • Missing a regulator: RBI (for NBFCs and CICs), CCI, or sector regulators can each add months.

Nuances that separate good from great

The details examiners and bankers like to probe. Each is small on its own; together they show you understand how a scheme actually runs.

Fractional entitlements

A ratio like 1 : 4 leaves odd holders with fractions. Schemes pool all fractions, a trustee sells them in the market, and holders get cash. A 1 : 1 ratio (Motherson, Jio Financial) avoids this entirely.

Practise: fractions

Tax cost and holding period

Under s.49(2C)/(2D) your original cost is split between parent and new company in proportion to net book value moved. The holding period of the new shares includes the time you held the parent, so long-term status carries over.

Practise: cost split

F&O contracts on the ex-date

Exchanges adjust derivatives when a large demerger goes ex. For Motherson, existing futures and options were made to expire early (13 Jan 2022) and fresh contracts started on the ex-date.

Price discovery on the ex-date

For big demergers, exchanges can hold a special pre-open session so the parent's price resets cleanly for the business that left. Jio Financial's 2023 demerger used one.

ESOPs and convertibles

Outstanding options are adjusted so holders aren't hurt: typically the resulting company grants matching options, and exercise prices are split in the same way as the tax cost.

Cross-holdings and treasury shares

No company issues shares to itself: a parent's stake in a merging subsidiary is cancelled, and a target's holding in the acquirer is extinguished. Older Indian schemes sometimes parked such shares in a trust ("treasury shares"); regulators now discourage this, so cancellation is the clean route.

Practise: cross-holdings

Appointed date choices

It can be a past date (to align accounts with a financial year) or tied to an event. MCA guidance (2019) says a date more than a year before filing needs justification. Motherson used 1 Apr 2021 for the demerger and "the day after the demerger completes" for the merger.

No exit right for dissenters

Unlike US appraisal rights, an Indian scheme gives dissenting shareholders no automatic cash-out. If the vote passes and the NCLT sanctions, they are bound. Only 10% shareholders or 5% creditors can even object.

Observation letter has a shelf life

The exchanges' no-objection is valid for a limited period (six months under SEBI's circular) for filing with the NCLT. Slow movers have to go back to the exchanges.

Demerger vs slump sale vs hive-down

A demerger hands shares to the parent's shareholders (tax-neutral if s.2(19AA) is met). A slump sale sells the business for cash (taxed). A hive-down moves it into a subsidiary the parent still owns, often as a first step before a later demerger or stake sale.

Stamp duty and land

Stamp duty is payable on the NCLT order under state laws and can be large where land moves. Leasing land to the new company instead (as Motherson did for MSWIL's plants) avoids transfer cost and delay on government-leased industrial plots.

Contracts, licences and lenders

Customer contracts, licences and permits must move with the undertaking. Lenders often have consent rights under loan covenants even when the NCLT dispenses with creditor meetings. Bankers start these conversations on day one.

Related-party flows after a demerger

Separated companies keep trading with each other. Caps, arm's-length pricing and annual audit committee review protect the new company's minorities. Motherson set caps such as components from Motherson ≤ 34.5% of MSWIL revenue and a management fee ≤ 1%.

Regulators beyond SEBI and NCLT

RBI for NBFCs and core investment companies (SAMIL was one), CCI for combinations above thresholds, IRDAI or TRAI in insurance or telecom, and FEMA rules for foreign shareholders receiving shares.

Open offer exemption

Shares acquired under an NCLT-sanctioned scheme are generally exempt from the SEBI takeover open offer (SAST Reg 10(1)(d)), provided conditions are met. This is why schemes are used to consolidate promoter holdings.

Practise: open offer check

The Indian demerger wave (pattern spotting)

Since 2022 many Indian groups have split conglomerates into focused listed companies. Recognising the pattern helps you answer "why now?" questions.

2022

Motherson → MSWIL

JV-partner-driven demerger plus promoter holdco merger.

2023

Reliance → Jio Financial

1 : 1 demerger of financial services; special price-discovery session.

2023

HDFC Ltd → HDFC Bank

Parent merged into the bank (42 : 25); its bank stake cancelled.

2024

Raymond → Raymond Lifestyle

Consumer business separated from real estate and engineering.

2025

ITC → ITC Hotels

1 : 10 demerger; ITC kept about 40%.

2025

Tata Motors → PV and CV companies

Passenger and commercial vehicle businesses split into two listed companies.

Why the wave?

Investors pay more for focused businesses than for conglomerates; separate companies can raise capital and do deals on their own terms; promoters can bring in partners at the business level; and schemes make the split tax-neutral. The risk, as MSWIL shows, is listing a "pure play" at a price that already assumes the re-rating.
