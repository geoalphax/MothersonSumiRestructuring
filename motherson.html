01

The story, in plain words

Start with an analogy and the real names will follow.

The two-shop analogy

You (the Sehgal family) and a Japanese friend (Sumitomo, called SWS) open a wiring shop together in 1986. That shop is MSSL. You put it on the stock market, so the public owns a piece too.

The shop does well and starts buying big businesses in Europe: car mirrors, bumpers and door panels. But when it buys the biggest one (SMRP BV), the shop takes only half (51%). Your own private family company, SAMIL, takes the other half (49%). SAMIL also owns about 28 other small businesses and holds your 33% stake in the shop.

By 2020 everyone is unhappy:

  • Your Japanese friend says: "I joined for wires in India. Why am I a part-owner of German bumper factories?"
  • The public says: "Why does the shop own only half the best business? Why does the family keep the other half privately? Whose side are you on?"
  • You want one clean, big listed company that you clearly control and can grow.

The fix comes in two moves. First, cut the Indian wiring business out into its own shop (MSWIL), so the Japanese friend owns a direct slice of exactly what he wants. Second, pour your private family company (SAMIL) into the main shop, so the main shop owns 100% of everything else and you own your stake directly.

Six names to remember

MSSL

Motherson Sumi Systems. The listed main company. Later renamed Samvardhana Motherson International.

SAMIL

The Sehgal family's private holding company. Unlisted. It owned 33.4% of MSSL and 49% of SMRP BV.

SWS

Sumitomo Wiring Systems, Japan. JV partner since 1986, with a 25.3% stake in MSSL.

SMRP BV

The European mirrors, bumpers and interiors business. The biggest earner, split 51% MSSL and 49% SAMIL.

DWH

Domestic Wiring Harness: wires for Indian vehicles. The original business and SWS's favourite.

MSWIL

Motherson Sumi Wiring India. The new company created to hold DWH.

03

Why demerge first, then merge?

This is the question sir will most likely ask. The short answer: the order decides who ends up owning the Indian wiring company. A demerger hands out shares of the new company to whoever owns MSSL at that moment, in the same proportions (a "mirror"). So the real question is: whose ownership do you want to copy?

  • Demerge first (what they did): copy MSSL's old ownership. SWS gets its full 25.3%, the public 38.3%, and SAMIL 33.4%. SAMIL's 33.4% then flows into the main company when SAMIL merges in.
  • Merge first (the alternative): SAMIL merges in, and the family's stake jumps to about 50% while SWS is diluted to about 18%. Copying that ownership into the wiring company would hand the family half the wiring company and shrink SWS's stake in its favourite business.
Who owns the new wiring company (MSWIL)?
MSWIL owners
PublicSWSSehgal family (direct)Main listed company (Motherson)Sojitz + SAMIL staff
Four reasons the order had to be "demerge, then merge"
  1. It protects SWS's stake in DWH. The whole deal was driven by SWS. Demerging first gives SWS 25.3% of the wiring company. Merging first would cut that to about 17.7%, which defeats the purpose.
  2. It keeps the wiring company inside the group. With demerger first, SAMIL's 33.4% of MSWIL moves into the main listed company. Motherson keeps a share of DWH's high-return cash flows (55% ROCE) and stays MSWIL's anchor shareholder.
  3. It stops the promoters taking a bigger direct slice of DWH. Merging first would leave the family owning about 50% of DWH directly. Instead, their look-through stake actually falls to about 20%.
  4. It makes the valuation cleaner. Once DWH is out, the valuers only need to compare two things: MSSL without DWH vs SAMIL. DWH is split 1:1 to everyone first, so nobody can argue about its price inside the swap ratio.
Why not just do one of the two?
  • Only the demerger? SWS is happy, but SMRP BV is still split 51/49 and SAMIL's 28 businesses stay outside. The minority shareholders' problem is unsolved.
  • Only the merger? Minorities are happy, but DWH stays buried inside a huge global company and SWS is simply diluted from 25% to 18% of businesses it never wanted. The JV partner walks away unhappy.
  • Both, in this order, is the only sequence that satisfies all three parties at once. That is what makes it a "composite" scheme: two steps, one approval.
Say it in one line

"A demerger copies the parent's shareholding at that moment. They demerged first so the wiring company copied the old shareholding, which gave SWS its full 25% and kept 33% inside the group. Then they merged SAMIL in to fix the main company."

04

The strategic rationale for each move

Move 1 · Demerger of DWH

Why carve out Indian wiring?

  • JV partner's demand. The press release calls it SWS's "long-standing request" to focus on Indian wiring.
  • Different business, different DNA. DWH: India only, about 55% ROCE, low capex, net cash. The rest: global, capex-heavy, acquisitive, around 8% EBITDA margin. Investors value them differently, so hiding a gem inside a big group lowers its value.
  • Management focus. DWH gets its own board, CEO and capital decisions.
  • Investor choice. Investors can choose a pure India story (MSWIL), a global story (Motherson), or both.
  • Continuity. Supply, technology and lease agreements keep the old benefits flowing (Lesson 09).
Move 2 · Merger of SAMIL

Why fold the family company in?

  • 100% of SMRP BV. All of its future profit goes to listed shareholders, not 51%.
  • No more "two pockets". The family no longer has businesses outside the listed company, which removes conflict of interest and the holding company discount.
  • A bigger product range. Lighting, HVAC, shock absorbers, metal parts and IT come in, supporting the 3CX10 diversification rule.
  • Synergies. SAMIL's shared services (IT, logistics, travel, legal) come in-house.
  • Growth platform. One large listed balance sheet can raise debt and equity for acquisitions (Vision 2025).
  • EPS accretive in year one, as promised.
Banker's framing: "Simplify · Align · Grow"

This is how the company sold it, and how you should structure any restructuring pitch. Simplify the structure (remove the holding company layer and the 51/49 split). Align every stakeholder's interest (SWS with DWH, promoters with minorities). Grow through a stronger platform (capital raising, M&A). Every slide in the investor deck maps to one of these three words.

05

The math, slowly

Move 1: the demerger is just "1 for 1"

If you held 100 MSSL shares, you got 100 MSWIL shares as well. You still had your 100 MSSL shares, but MSSL no longer owned the wiring business. Your ₹ value is split across two pieces of paper instead of one.

Why 1:1 is automatically fair: every shareholder gets the same ratio, so everyone's percentage of every business stays the same. 1:2 or 5:1 would be equally fair, because only the price per share would change. 1:1 is simply the cleanest, so MSWIL also has 3,157.9 mn shares.

Fixed inputs from the valuation reports and FAQs: MSSL ex-DWH worth ₹31,930 cr (₹101.1 per share); public held 1,208.6 mn shares worth ₹12,220 cr before; merged Q3 FY21 pro forma profit ₹830 cr. "Fair ratio" is the ratio at which public holders neither gain nor lose under your value assumption. The ratio can't sensibly go below ~2.23: that is SAMIL's 1,055.8 mn MSSL shares ÷ 473.6 mn SAMIL shares, i.e. SAMIL's other assets valued at zero.

What the simulator teaches

A swap ratio is just a price, paid in shares instead of cash. If it is too high, the family gets too many shares and value leaks from the public to the promoters. If it is too low, the opposite. Each ₹1,000 cr that SAMIL's assets are overvalued moves about ₹268 cr from public holders to SAMIL's owners. The ratio also nearly decides control: at 5.1, the family lands just above 50%.

How 5.1 was decided

Three independent valuers each valued one MSSL share (without DWH) and one SAMIL share, then divided the two. Think of it like comparing the price of a kilo of apples and a kilo of mangoes: you only need the relative price to know how many apples buy one mango.

ValuerMSSL ex-DWH (₹/share)SAMIL (₹/share)SAMIL ÷ MSSL
Price Waterhouse & Co100.51512.815.10
BSR & Associates101.81519.275.10
Incwert (registered valuer)1015165.11
Final ratio51 : 10

Method: sum of the parts

Value every business separately (DWH, SMRP BV, export wiring, each SAMIL JV), then add them up and subtract debt. Used because the businesses are very different from each other.

50% DCF

Discount five years of projected cash flow (FY21 to FY25). This captures the expected recovery of SMRP BV's new plants.

50% peer multiples

What similar listed companies trade at (EV/EBITDA, P/E). This anchors the value to the market.

0% book value

Calculated (₹34 vs ₹148) but ignored, because these are running businesses, not companies being liquidated.

Banker's view: valuer vs fairness opinion

The valuer (PwC, BSR, Incwert) recommends the ratio. A merchant banker then gives a fairness opinion saying "this ratio is fair, from a financial point of view, to the shareholders". Axis Capital and DSP Merrill Lynch (BofA) gave opinions to MSSL, and Kotak to SAMIL. One detail worth quoting: DSP Merrill's opinion says the ratio is fair to MSSL holders "other than SAMIL and the Sehgal family". That is exactly the group a fairness opinion should protect in a promoter deal.

08

The scheme of arrangement journey

A scheme of arrangement (Companies Act 2013, Sections 230 to 232) is a court-approved plan to reorganise companies. Once the NCLT (the company law tribunal) sanctions it, it binds every shareholder and creditor, including those who voted against. No one has to sign a share purchase agreement and no cash changes hands.

Why use a scheme here?

(1) Shares, not cash. (2) Tax-neutral: demerger under Income Tax Act s.2(19AA), amalgamation under s.2(1B). (3) Exempt from the SEBI open offer the promoters' jump to 50% would otherwise trigger (SAST Reg 10(1)(d)). (4) Both steps approved in one go.

Votes needed

Companies Act: a majority in number holding three-fourths in value of those voting. SEBI adds a majority of minority test when a promoter entity is involved: public "for" votes must exceed public "against" votes, so the promoters can't push it through alone.

What the bankers actually did, step by step

  1. Structuring: options paper, sequencing (demerge then merge), tax and SEBI checks. The FAQ says several banks pitched structures over the years.
  2. Valuation coordination: data room, management projections, diligence reports to valuers, then the fairness opinion.
  3. Equity story: the 65-page investor deck ("Poised for Next Phase of Growth"), the "Simplify, Align, Grow" message, and pro forma financials.
  4. Handling pushback: when the stock fell, publishing FAQs with the look-through EBITDA table, the 8% EPS accretion and the greenfield turnaround before the vote (February 2021). This is a textbook way to win a majority-of-minority vote.
  5. Governance optics: promoter directors abstaining, independent audit committee, RPT approval sought voluntarily, 1-year lock-in.
  6. Execution: exchange filings, NCLT motions, record date, listing of MSWIL.
  7. After the deal: SWS's block sales in 2023 and 2024 and the 2024 ₹6,438 cr QIP were all banker-run trades that the new structure made possible.
If you were the banker for minority shareholders, you would push for…
  • Fixing the ratio on normalised (ex-greenfield) earnings, or adding a true-up if SMRP BV's recovery came faster than planned.
  • A longer promoter lock-in than one year.
  • Hard caps and annual independent review of the MSWIL ↔ Motherson related-party deals (Lesson 09 explains why).
  • A fairness opinion addressed only to public holders from a bank with no other mandate from the group.

Sir's assignment, answered

The brief asks for "a short presentation" covering four points. Here is a ready answer for each, with the slides to make.

1. Core reasons why the restructuring was initiated

Driven by the JV partner: SWS wanted to focus on Indian wiring harnesses. Minority pressure: MSSL owned only 51% of SMRP BV while the promoter entity owned 49% plus 28 businesses, creating conflicts and a holding company discount. Promoters: they wanted direct control and one platform for growth. Business logic: DWH (India, about 55% ROCE, low capex) and the global business needed different strategies and capital.

Slides: (1) group before, with one simple diagram; (2) three stakeholders and three problems.

2. The math behind the restructuring

Step 1: 1:1 demerger, so MSWIL has 3,157.9 mn shares with mirror ownership. Step 2: 51:10 merger, so 3,157.9 − 1,055.8 (cancelled) + 2,415.4 (new) = 4,517.6 mn shares, 43% net dilution. Ownership moves to family 50.4% / public 31.9% / SWS 17.7%. Look-through: the public's stake rises in DWH (38.3 → 47.2%), SMRP BV (19.5 → 26.8%) and SAMIL businesses (0 → 26.8%). EBITDA accretion +3.1% (FY20); EPS accretion +8% (Q3 FY21).

Slides: (3) the two steps with before/after charts; (4) share-count bridge and look-through table.

3. Valuation of both entities and the resulting valuation

SOTP, 50% DCF + 50% comparable multiples, NAV not used. MSSL ex-DWH ≈ ₹101 per share (₹31,930 cr); SAMIL ≈ ₹516 per share (₹24,440 cr), of which ₹10,675 cr is its MSSL stake and ₹13,765 cr is its other assets. Ratio 5.1. Merged value ≈ ₹45,695 cr, still ₹101 per share. Market check: MSSL market cap ₹32,700 cr at announcement; after the split, MSWIL listed at ₹20,800 cr. Today: Motherson ₹1.71 lakh cr + MSWIL ₹0.23 lakh cr.

Slides: (5) valuers' table and method; (6) value bridge and then-vs-now market cap.

4. Your post-facto view: was it successful, and why?

Use the scorecard in Lesson 13 and the 30-second verdict. Successful structurally and for shareholder returns (about 4.2x vs 2.2x Nifty). SWS's later sell-down proves the separation was what it wanted. Caveats: the rich pricing of SAMIL at the trough, MSWIL's flat stock, Vision 2025 missed, and the new related-party conflict at MSWIL.

Teach it to someone else

Whiteboard: draw 3 pictures
  1. Before: two boxes, MSSL and SAMIL. Draw SMRP BV split under both (51/49). Write "SWS 25%" on MSSL.
  2. Move 1: pull a green "DWH" box out of MSSL into a new box, MSWIL. Copy MSSL's owners onto it.
  3. Move 2: draw an arrow pouring SAMIL into MSSL. Cross out SAMIL's 33% of MSSL. Write "family 50%".
The 60-second pitch

"Motherson's JV partner Sumitomo wanted only Indian wiring, and investors hated that the promoters privately owned half of the best business. So in 2020 they did two moves in one court scheme. First, they demerged Indian wiring into MSWIL, giving every shareholder 1-for-1 shares so Sumitomo kept 25%. Then they merged the family company SAMIL into Motherson at 51-for-10, so Motherson owns 100% of SMRP BV and the family owns 50% directly. No cash, no tax, no open offer, and 99% minority approval. Shareholders made about 4x since."

Three ideas that unlock the whole case
  1. A demerger copies ownership. Whoever owns the parent on the record date owns the child in the same proportion. That is why the order matters.
  2. A swap ratio is a price paid in shares. It is fair only if both sides are valued correctly. Too high a ratio means value moves to the promoters.
  3. Look through to the businesses. Don't judge a deal by % in one company; ask what slice of each business each person ends up with.
